## Journey overview

### 1 Prepare the destination before departure

In the linked **2024 notional mission sequence**, cargo and landing elements are deployed before the crew arrives. The earlier overview described habitats, power equipment, supplies, and equipment for leaving Mars as examples of advance infrastructure. **Pending original-source verification:** which of those items this particular sequence actually prepositions, their deployment order, and what must be operational before crew departure. Do not treat the illustrative cargo list as a confirmed manifest. [Notional mission sequence](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240005900/downloads/EIMO_TIM5_21May2024_FINAL.pdf)

### 2 Launch and check the spacecraft near Earth

In the **crew-operations study**, the crew reaches Earth orbit, prepares the transit habitat and integrated vehicle, and checks systems before departure. The overview envisaged docking with a larger transit spacecraft and activating life support and propulsion. **Pending original-source verification:** exact docking sequence, assembly location, and checkout requirements. Additional assembly or refueling launches are architecture-dependent possibilities from the synthesis, not established requirements of that study. [Crew-operations study](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230012629/downloads/Overview%20of%20Crew%20Operations%20for%20Transit%20to%20Mars%20V5.pdf)

### 3 Depart Earth on a timed trajectory

As a **general orbital-mechanics explanation**, propulsion places the spacecraft on a Sun-centered trajectory that meets Mars at its future position. For conventional transfers, favorable opportunities recur roughly every 26 months. **Pending original-source verification:** the distinction between opposition recurrence and usable departure windows, and the trajectory and propulsion assumptions behind any specific departure. The earlier reference to “an engine burn” simplifies a sequence that may involve multiple maneuvers. [Trajectory explanation](https://science.nasa.gov/learn/basics-of-space-flight/chapter4-1/), [Earth–Mars alignment](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/hubble/mars-closest-encounter/)

### 4 Live and work during the cruise

The **general NASA journey overview** describes travel taking roughly six to nine months. That range must not be assigned automatically to the **2024 notional sequence** or to every propulsion option. **Pending original-source verification:** the applicable trajectory, departure opportunity, and duration for each concept. [Journey overview](https://www.nasa.gov/general/what-it-takes-to-get-us-to-mars-and-back-featuring-patrick-chai/)

The **crew-operations study** describes vehicle monitoring and maintenance, training for arrival and surface operations, and maintaining crew health. The **human-research overview** identifies radiation, isolation, distance from Earth, and altered gravity as hazards. Daily life would likely include exercise, health monitoring, experiments, and equipment work. **Pending original-source verification:** the detailed schedule, habitat assumptions, and allocation of those activities. [Crew-operations study](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230012629/downloads/Overview%20of%20Crew%20Operations%20for%20Transit%20to%20Mars%20V5.pdf), [Human-spaceflight hazards](https://www.nasa.gov/ochmo-hmta-human-spaceflight-hazards/)

The **communications analysis** describes delays that depend on trajectory and planetary positions, with one-way delays potentially exceeding 20 minutes in some phases. The resulting need for onboard handling of urgent problems is an operational inference in this synthesis. **Pending original-source verification:** delay profiles and the source's specific autonomy requirements; the maximum must not be presented as the delay throughout outbound cruise. [Communications analysis](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/mars-communications-disruption-and-delay.pdf)

### 5 Prepare for arrival and reach Mars

The **2024 notional sequence** has the deep-space transport enter Mars orbit and dock with a lander, followed by crew descent. The earlier overview also mentioned direct arrival as an alternative. **Pending original-source verification:** orbital capture method, transfer sequence, and lander details. The direct-entry alternative was not tied to an original source in the overview and remains an **unverified alternative**, not part of the cited orbit-first sequence. [Notional mission sequence](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240005900/downloads/EIMO_TIM5_21May2024_FINAL.pdf)

### 6 Enter the atmosphere and land

In **human-scale entry, descent, and landing research**, atmospheric drag and thermal protection reduce entry speed and protect the vehicle. Mars' thin atmosphere makes heavy-payload landing difficult; the cited research investigates engine braking at supersonic speeds because conventional parachute approaches do not scale adequately to the vehicles considered. **Pending original-source verification:** vehicle mass, aerodynamic design, heat-shield assumptions, engine ignition conditions, and applicability to the separate 2024 mission sequence. This research does not establish that a crew-rated system has been demonstrated. [Human-scale landing research](https://www.nas.nasa.gov/SC21/research/project27.html)

The overview ends with a precise, largely autonomous touchdown near essential equipment. That is a **synthesis of operational needs**, not a verified landing specification for any one concept. Landing accuracy, hazard avoidance, automation, and distance to supplies need architecture-specific evidence.

## Working conclusion

The illustrative sequence is launch, preparation near Earth, departure, months of transit, Mars arrival, and landing. Its duration, vehicles, cargo sequence, and landing methods cannot yet be combined into one internally consistent mission design. Success would require the crew, life support, and destination infrastructure to remain usable throughout the sequence.
